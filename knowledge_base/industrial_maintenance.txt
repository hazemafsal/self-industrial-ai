
INDUSTRIAL MACHINE MAINTENANCE KNOWLEDGE BASE

1. PREDICTIVE MAINTENANCE

Predictive maintenance uses sensor measurements, machine history,
inspection records, and statistical or machine-learning models to
identify possible maintenance needs before a failure occurs.

A prediction is not proof that a failure will occur. Decisions should
consider sensor reliability, operating conditions, maintenance history,
and the consequences of a missed failure.

2. AIR AND PROCESS TEMPERATURE

Air temperature and process temperature describe different thermal
conditions. Their relationship depends on machine design, operating
state, sensor placement, and ambient conditions.

Unexpected readings should be checked against the equipment manual,
sensor calibration records, and recent operating history. A single
temperature value should not be used as a universal safety limit.

3. ROTATIONAL SPEED

Rotational speed is commonly measured in revolutions per minute (rpm).
An unexpected reduction, increase, or zero reading may reflect a
stopped machine, a change in operating mode, a sensor issue, or a fault.

Compare readings with the specified operating range for the particular
machine and production task.

4. TORQUE

Torque describes rotational force and is often measured in newton-metres
(Nm). Unusual torque may be associated with a changing load, friction,
material differences, tool wear, or sensor problems.

Use machine-specific limits and historical baselines. Do not treat a
generic threshold as an approved shutdown limit.

5. TOOL WEAR

Tool wear represents accumulated use or a wear measurement, depending
on the equipment and data source. Wear may affect quality, load, and
maintenance requirements.

Inspect the tool according to the manufacturer's schedule and validated
maintenance procedures. Do not rely solely on an AI score to decide
whether a tool must be replaced.

6. DATA QUALITY

Before trusting a prediction, check that:
- sensor readings use the correct units;
- measurements are recent and timestamped;
- sensors are calibrated and functioning;
- machine identifiers and product types are correct;
- values are within the machine's documented operating range;
- missing values and communication interruptions are identified;
- model inputs resemble the data used for training.

Unusual data may represent a real fault, a new operating condition, or
a measurement problem. Investigate before drawing a conclusion.

7. MACHINE FAILURE PREDICTION

A machine-learning model estimates patterns associated with failure
labels in its training data. Performance must be assessed on held-out
data and, where possible, on later data and separate machines.

Accuracy alone can be misleading when failures are rare. Review recall
for failures, precision, the confusion matrix, balanced accuracy,
precision-recall performance, and the costs of false alarms and missed
failures.

A model probability is not automatically a calibrated real-world
failure probability. Calibration must be measured on representative
data.

8. ANOMALY DETECTION

An anomaly detector flags observations that differ from patterns in its
training data. An anomaly does not necessarily mean a machine is
failing. A normal-looking observation does not guarantee safety.

Investigate unusual readings using independent sensor checks, operating
logs, inspection findings, and approved maintenance procedures.

9. WHEN A PREDICTION MAY BE UNRELIABLE

Treat a prediction cautiously when:
- input readings are missing or implausible;
- sensors may be faulty or miscalibrated;
- the operating condition differs from the training data;
- a novelty detector flags an unusual pattern;
- model performance on the current machine is unknown;
- the model or data pipeline has changed;
- the consequences of a false negative are significant.

An anomaly score is not the probability that the model is wrong.
Evaluate prediction reliability using labeled outcomes, calibration,
distribution-shift monitoring, and validation on new data.

10. SAFETY AND ESCALATION

This knowledge base is general guidance, not a machine-specific
operating procedure. Follow the manufacturer's documentation, site
safety rules, and authorized engineering instructions.

Do not disable guards, bypass interlocks, or change safety controls
based on a language-model recommendation. If a serious fault is
suspected, follow the site's approved escalation and shutdown process.
